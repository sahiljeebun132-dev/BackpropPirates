# Decision log

Key decisions with times. Friday 13 – Saturday 14 November 2026, Mauritius time.

| Time | Decision | Why | Alternatives considered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fri 12:27 | Created the public repo `BackpropPirates` | Rule 2: own public repo from kickoff | — |
| Fri 13:33 | Automatic hourly checkpoint commits, on top of manual commits after each feature | Judges check commit history; organisers asked for every 1–2 hours | Manual commits only; every 3 hours |
| Fri 14:05 | Stack: mobile-first web app (Vite, React, TypeScript, Tailwind) on Vercel | Opens on any phone from a link, with no APK build that can fail before the demo. Organisers confirmed web apps count equally | Expo/React Native APK; Flutter |
| Fri 14:20 | Theme 2, Powered to Move. Problem: commuters cannot see how dangerous a specific journey is | Mauritius publishes rich road-casualty data that reaches nobody who is about to travel | Theme 1 scam checker; Theme 3 clinic finder |
| Fri 14:30 | User: a young two-wheeler rider on the northern corridor, riding after dark | 49.9% of casualties are riders or pedestrians; the worst district is on that corridor; darkness more than doubles the fatal share. A specific user, not "everyone in Mauritius" | A general driver audience, which the organisers named as a classic mistake |
| Fri 14:40 | Core dataset: Accident Prone Areas, because it holds real road geometry | It is the difference between "this district is risky" and "your route passes 14 named black spots". Turned a generic idea into a corridor-specific one | District averages only |
| Fri 14:45 | Fetch data via the Electric Sheep mirror rather than scraping data.govmu.org | The portal is behind a bot check. The mirror carries the original CKAN resource IDs and URLs, so provenance survives and the app links to the original file | Manual browser downloads, which nobody could reproduce |
| Fri 15:00 | Derive town coordinates from official health-facility positions | Even the place list is then data-derived rather than typed in | A hand-written list of town coordinates |
| Fri 15:05 | Map the 9 administrative districts onto the 7 police districts ourselves, and publish the mapping | The casualty table and the facilities table use different district schemes. Better to state the assumption than to hide it | Dropping the district factor entirely |
| Fri 15:20 | Score shows its own arithmetic: each factor opens to its figures and its source table | "Where does 31 come from" is the first question a judge asks. Answer it in the app, not in the pitch | A single opaque number |
| Fri 15:35 | Rebuilt the score's normalisation after testing all 5,200 combinations | The first model rated 70% of journeys Severe and nothing safe, because vehicle occupants are always the largest casualty group, so every car trip was punished for being ordinary. Anchoring each factor to both ends of the observed range fixed it | Shipping it and hoping no judge tried a rural daytime route |
| Fri 15:50 | Keep a build journal as a generated Word document | So any team member can answer a jury question with the same figures the app shows, and it regenerates from the live data | Notes in a chat thread |
| Fri 15:05 | Named the app **Zetway** | Short, memorable and ours. "Zet" reads as the Creole/French clip of *trajet* — the journey — which is exactly what the app scores. The team stays BackpropPirates | SafeRoute Mauritius, which was descriptive but generic |
| Fri 15:15 | Adopted the Zetway logo and rebuilt the app palette from it | The mark carries the Mauritius flag inside a location pin, which says "this is about this island" before a word is read. The app's near-black header is the logo's own background so the mark sits flush, the blue is sampled from WAY, and the alert colours are the flag's own red, yellow and green | Keeping the generic teal |
| Fri 15:25 | Rejected live satellite crash detection | Seeing something car-sized needs sub-metre resolution, which means low Earth orbit and roughly one pass a day. The satellites that watch continuously are weather satellites at 500 m to 2 km a pixel, where a car does not exist. Mauritius is also frequently cloudy. No constellation gives both resolution and continuity | Tasking commercial imagery (hours of lead time, hundreds of dollars a shot) |
| Fri 15:30 | Live conditions from Open-Meteo instead, free and keyless | Weather models ingest satellite and radar observations, so this is remote-sensed data that actually arrives hourly. Sunrise and sunset let us work out the light band from a real departure time rather than asking the rider to classify it | A paid traffic API, which needs an account and whose Mauritius probe coverage is unproven |
| Fri 15:40 | Rain informs advice but does not move the score | Pooled 2013-2018, rain lifts the fatal share from 4.67% to 5.88%, a ratio of 1.26. Real, but too small to swing a score, and only 8% of casualty accidents happen in the wet. Overstating it would be the same sin as inventing a number | Adding weather as a fifth scored factor |
| Fri 15:45 | No automatic police notification | A false positive sends officers to nothing, we have no integration or authority, and the rules ask us to help people reach services rather than investigate. The app already offers one-tap calling to the nearest station | Auto-dispatch on a detected incident |
| Fri 16:00 | Hazard reports from riders, on a private Vercel Blob store | People on the road are the sensor a satellite cannot be. Blob could be provisioned from the CLI, so it needed no credentials from anyone and no key reaches the browser | Supabase, which needed keys we could not fetch ourselves |
| Fri 16:05 | One blob per report rather than one shared JSON file | Two riders reporting in the same second would have overwritten each other with a single file. Per-report objects cannot race | A single reports.json, simpler but lossy |
| Fri 16:10 | Reports show above the score but never change it | The score measures recorded exposure over years and cites government tables. An unverified report from a stranger is a different kind of claim, and quietly folding it in would undermine the thing that makes the score defensible | Letting a crash report drop the score |
| Fri 17:55 | Ship an Android APK **as well as** the web app, from one codebase, via Capacitor | The web app stays the guaranteed demo: it opens from a link on any phone and cannot fail to install. The APK is additive, and it is the only way to get the three things a browser forbids. Same React code, so there is no second app to keep correct | Expo/React Native rewrite, which would have meant rebuilding the whole UI with 18 hours left; a Trusted Web Activity, which is still Chrome and unlocks nothing |
| Fri 18:05 | After a crash the Android build texts every contact and rings the first one, with no tap | This is the promise the web build could not keep. A page may not send or call on its own, by design, because one that could would be a gift to every scam site. Natively both are ordinary Android, so the countdown finishing is the last thing that needs a human | Leaving everything one-tap, which is useless to someone unconscious |
| Fri 18:10 | 114 and 999 stay one tap, even in the native app | Not our choice, and worth saying out loud: Android refuses `ACTION_CALL` for emergency numbers from any third-party app, redirects it to the preloaded dialler, and `TelecomManager.placeCall` accepts one only from the default dialler. `CALL_PRIVILEGED` would lift it and is closed to third-party apps. So no app on the Play Store can auto-dial an ambulance. The plugin asks the OS whether a number is an emergency number and refuses rather than failing at the worst moment | Hard-coding 114 past the check, which would throw a SecurityException mid-crash |
| Fri 18:12 | The automatic call goes to the first saved contact, not to 114 | Given the rule above, the useful automatic call is to a person who can act. They hear the ambient audio, they already have the GPS link by text, and they can ring 114 themselves. A number the rider must tap helps nobody who is unconscious | Opening the dialler at 114 and hoping |
| Fri 19:05 | Crash watch moved into an Android foreground service | Until this, the feature only worked with the app open and the screen lit, which is not how anyone carries a phone on a bike — so it would have demoed well and helped nobody. A service plus a partial wake lock watches a phone in a pocket. The wake lock is not optional: a foreground service keeps the process alive but lets the CPU doze, and sensor callbacks stop arriving | Leaving it foreground-only and disclosing the limit; a periodic WorkManager job, which cannot sample a sensor fast enough to catch an impact |
| Fri 19:10 | The service detects, the web layer still decides | The countdown, alarm, texts and call stay in one place rather than being written twice in Java. The service's only job is to stay alive, watch one number, and get the app in front of the rider | Reimplementing the whole escalation natively |
| Fri 19:15 | The alert arrives as a full-screen-intent notification | An app in the background may not simply start an activity on Android 10 and later. A full-screen intent is the mechanism alarm clocks and incoming calls use, and it is the only reliable way to raise a screen on a locked, dark phone. We attempt startActivity as well, and the system declines it when it must | Trusting startActivity alone, which fails silently exactly when it matters |
| Fri 19:20 | Declared the service as `specialUse`, not `health` or `location` | Android 14 requires a foreground service type, and none of the predefined ones describes crash detection. `health` would have been a convenient lie to the operating system. `specialUse` with the subtype spelled out is the truthful declaration | Claiming `health`, which is enforced by Play policy rather than at runtime and would have worked |
| Fri 19:40 | The crash alert uses an alarm sound at alarm volume, not a notification | A notification blip at notification volume is inaudible to someone face-down on a road, and a phone on silent plays it not at all — whereas a phone on silent still rings for an alarm. `USAGE_ALARM` plus `setBypassDnd` is what an alarm clock does, and it is the right precedent | The default notification sound |
| Fri 19:42 | Versioned the alert channel id to `.v2` | A notification channel is immutable once created. Changing its sound or lock-screen visibility in code does nothing at all on a phone that already has the old one — the change would have silently applied only to fresh installs, which is the worst kind of "fixed" | Editing the existing channel and assuming it took |
| Fri 19:45 | The app checks whether it can actually wake a locked screen, and says so | Android 14 reserved full-screen intents for calling and alarm apps. Denied, our alert is quietly downgraded to a heads-up notification, which a locked screen never shows — detection would work perfectly and nobody would be told. There is no runtime prompt for it, so the app checks `canUseFullScreenIntent` and offers the settings screen | Assuming it works, and finding out after a crash |
| Fri 19:48 | The app offers to exempt itself from battery optimisation | Without the exemption a great many phones, and nearly every Chinese OEM skin, kill a foreground service after minutes in a pocket — exactly the case the service exists for. An offer the rider can accept beats a limitation buried in a README | Documenting it and hoping riders read it |
| Fri 19:50 | A readiness checklist above the arm button, not a paragraph of caveats | Three separate settings decide whether an alert reaches a locked phone, all the rider's to grant. A crash is the worst possible moment to discover one was off, so the app shows the state of each with a button to fix it | Prose warnings nobody reads |
| Fri 19:20 | Ask for the crash permissions when the app opens, not when they are used | A real fault found by walking the app: SEND_SMS and CALL_PHONE were requested at the moment of the escalation, so the first thing that would have happened after a crash was a permission dialog in front of someone possibly unable to answer it. Everything after it would silently not have happened. Asked up front, with the reason on screen first rather than four dialogs out of nowhere | Per-use prompts, which read as polite and were the worst possible choice here |
| Fri 19:25 | Fixed: the garage panel claimed it had failed when it had never been asked to load | The panel's render condition and its `enabled` condition had drifted apart, so on the car-details step it appeared with an empty list and said "the garage list has not loaded". Loading state, failure and a genuinely empty area are three different things and now read as three different messages. The compiler then showed the stage test was redundant once the conditions matched | Leaving one message for three states |
| Fri 19:30 | The crash walkthrough is a self-contained web page, not a Blender render | We have Blender, and 3D was the first instinct. But a rendered animation cannot be checked quickly, costs render time we do not have before a pitch, and is frozen the moment a number changes. This page is driven by the app's own constants, replays instantly, has a real-time toggle so a judge can watch the full 30 seconds, and has no fonts or scripts from anywhere — it runs with the venue wifi off, which is the failure mode that actually bites at hackathons | A Blender render; a screen recording, which cannot be paused and questioned |
| Fri 19:35 | The walkthrough draws the scene from elapsed time every frame, not inside each event's window | Found while testing: with frames dropped the rider never visibly crashed, because the tumble was written once mid-animation and never again. A projector or a loaded laptop is exactly where frames go missing, so the one thing the room came to see was the thing most likely to vanish | Trusting that every frame lands |
| Fri 20:05 | Built the 3D crash sequence in Blender after all, scripted rather than modelled | The objection to Blender was that a render cannot be checked quickly. That turned out to be wrong here: Blender is installed, so it can be driven headless and single frames rendered and looked at, which is a real iteration loop. Scripted rather than hand-modelled so the timing stays tied to the app's constants, and so the whole thing can be rebuilt from one file | Hand-modelling in the GUI, which nobody could reproduce or re-time |
| Fri 20:10 | Four bugs found by rendering frames rather than trusting the script | The rider was parented to the bike, so it rotated with the machine instead of coming off it and ended under the road. The phone landed face-down, so the red screen never showed. The screen plate was a child of the phone body and inherited its scale, rendering as a speck of light rather than a lit face. And the first lighting pass was lit like real darkness, which on a projector is a black rectangle. None of these would have been visible without looking at the output | Shipping the script unrendered |
